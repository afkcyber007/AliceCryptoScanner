A setup that looks perfect on the 15-minute chart can be a tiny bounce inside a powerful daily downtrend. **Top-down analysis** solves this by reading the market from the biggest picture to the smallest: first the trend, then the setup, then the entry. It is the routine most professional traders follow every day.

![Top-down analysis from daily trend to 4-hour pullback, 1-hour change of structure and 15-minute entry](img:edu/top_down.webp "Daily trend up, 4H pullback into support, 1H structure turns up, 15M gives the entry trigger.")

## The three roles of timeframes

| Role | Typical timeframe | Question it answers |
|---|---|---|
| Trend | Daily (or weekly) | Which direction should I trade? |
| Setup | 4-hour and 1-hour | Where is a good area to trade from? |
| Entry | 15-minute (or 5-minute) | When exactly do I get in, and where is my stop? |

A useful rule is to keep a ratio of about 4 to 6 between timeframes: daily, 4-hour, 1-hour, 15-minute.

## Step 1: Read the trend on the daily chart

Mark the market structure: higher highs and higher lows for an uptrend, lower highs and lower lows for a downtrend. Note the 50 and 200-day moving averages and the major support and resistance zones. Write down your bias: long, short, or stay out.

## Step 2: Find the setup on the 4-hour and 1-hour

In an uptrend, you want to buy pullbacks, not chase highs. Look for price coming back into:

- A support zone or a broken resistance (break and retest).
- The 50% to 61.8% Fibonacci retracement of the last swing.
- An order block or fair value gap.
- The 21 or 50 EMA.

When several of these line up in one area, that area is your setup zone.

## Step 3: Time the entry on the 15-minute

Inside the setup zone, the lower timeframe will often still be falling. Wait for it to turn: a break of the last lower high (a change of character), followed by a higher low. Enter on that higher low, with the stop below it or below the zone.

## Why this improves your trading

- **Better direction:** you only trade with the dominant trend.
- **Smaller stops:** the entry timeframe gives a precise stop, so the position can be larger for the same risk, or the risk smaller for the same size.
- **Bigger targets:** targets come from the higher timeframe, so a 15-minute entry can aim for a 4-hour level. That is how 1:3 and 1:5 trades happen.

## Common mistakes

- **Too many timeframes.** Three or four are enough. Looking at seven charts creates confusion.
- **Letting the entry chart change your bias.** If the 15-minute turns bearish but the daily is bullish, that is a pullback, not a reversal.
- **Ignoring the session.** A great setup at 3 a.m. on a quiet market can drift for hours. Time entries for active sessions.

## Frequently asked questions

### Which timeframes should a day trader use?

A common set is the 4-hour for trend, the 1-hour for setups and the 5- or 15-minute for entries.

### Which timeframes should a swing trader use?

Weekly or daily for trend, daily and 4-hour for setups, and the 1-hour for entries.

### What if the timeframes disagree?

Then the market is in transition. The safest choice is to wait until the setup timeframe aligns with the trend timeframe.
