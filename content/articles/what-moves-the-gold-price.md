Gold has no earnings, pays no dividend and has no central bank of its own. So why does it move, sometimes by more than 2% in a day? Because its price reflects what investors think about interest rates, the dollar, inflation and risk. Understanding these drivers helps you know when gold has the wind at its back and when it is fighting it.

## 1. Real interest rates

![Gold trend with moving averages](img:art/ma_crossover.webp "Dollar, yields and risk sentiment drive gold's big trends.")

The single most important driver over the long run is the **real interest rate**: the yield on bonds after inflation. In the US it is often tracked with the yield on 10-year Treasury Inflation-Protected Securities (TIPS).

Holding gold means giving up the interest you could earn on bonds or cash. When real yields are high, that cost is high and gold tends to struggle. When real yields fall or turn negative, holding gold costs little and demand rises.

This is why gold reacts so strongly to Federal Reserve expectations. Anything that suggests lower rates in the future, like weak jobs data or falling inflation, is usually good for gold.

## 2. The US dollar

Gold is priced in dollars worldwide. When the dollar strengthens, gold becomes more expensive for buyers using other currencies, and its dollar price tends to fall. When the dollar weakens, gold usually rises.

Traders watch the **US Dollar Index (DXY)**, which measures the dollar against a basket of major currencies. Most of the time gold and DXY move in opposite directions. When both rise together, it often signals strong safe-haven demand, which is worth paying attention to.

## 3. Inflation

Gold has a reputation as an inflation hedge. Over long periods it has tended to hold its value as money loses purchasing power. In the short term the link is less direct: high inflation can push central banks to raise rates, which can hurt gold. What matters most is inflation compared with interest rates, which brings us back to real yields.

## 4. Fear and safe-haven demand

In crises, investors buy what they trust. Wars, banking failures, sharp stock market falls and political shocks have all triggered strong gold rallies. These moves can be fast and large, and they can briefly override the normal relationship with yields and the dollar.

## 5. Central banks and big buyers

Central banks hold gold as part of their reserves. In recent years they have bought gold at a historically high pace, with several countries adding to their holdings to reduce reliance on the dollar. This steady buying supports the price and helped gold reach record highs in 2024 and 2025.

Demand for jewellery, especially in China and India, and flows into gold ETFs also matter, though they move the price more slowly.

## 6. US economic data and the Fed

Day to day, the biggest moves often come from scheduled US events:

| Event | Usual timing | Typical reaction |
|---|---|---|
| Non-farm payrolls | First Friday of the month, 8:30 New York | Strong jobs: gold down. Weak jobs: gold up. |
| CPI inflation | Mid-month, 8:30 New York | Hot inflation: often gold down on rate fears |
| FOMC decision | Eight times a year, 14:00 New York | Dovish: gold up. Hawkish: gold down. |
| Retail sales, GDP, PMIs | Monthly or quarterly | Smaller moves in the same direction as above |

These are usual reactions, not rules. The surprise compared with forecasts matters more than the number itself, and the first spike is often reversed.

## Frequently asked questions

### Why do gold and the dollar move in opposite directions?

Because gold is priced in dollars. A stronger dollar makes gold more expensive in other currencies, which reduces demand, and vice versa.

### Does gold always rise in a crisis?

Often, but not always. In a sudden crash investors sometimes sell gold to raise cash, before buying it back as the crisis develops.

### Is gold a good inflation hedge?

Over decades it has tended to keep its purchasing power, but in any single year the link can be weak, because interest rates and the dollar matter more in the short run.
